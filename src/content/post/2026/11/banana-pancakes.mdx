When I was a kid in the 90s, my family had a code word. If there was an emergency and a grandparent, an aunt or uncle, a cousin or a family friend had to pick me up instead of my parents, they had to say it. This was before cell phones, even before pagers in a lot of ways. The code word told me it was a safe pickup.

I think every family needs one again. Something silly, like banana pancakes.

Here's why. In just the past two weeks, Bell in Canada was [breached by what it called an "agentic" threat actor](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/business/company-news/2026/11/23/bell-investigating-cyberattack-that-exposed-customer-information-last-week/), and customer data leaked. 1Password, one of the companies we trust with our passwords, [had customer names and emails stolen](https://www.newsbeep.com/826187/). The CIA had a breach. The FAA [grounded every flight in the US](https://www.faa.gov/newsroom/nationwide-ground-stop-questions-and-answers) for more than four hours on November 13, then [United and Delta had their own outages](https://www.thetraveler.org/thanksgiving-flyers-face-fresh-tech-jitters-after-faa-and-airline-outages/) days later. The FAA says there's no evidence of a cyberattack. Alex Stamos, CISO at Cognition, said on the [Search Engine podcast](https://www.searchengine.show/should-i-take-my-money-out-of-the-bank-how-we-got-to-this-cybersecurity-crisis) that many recent outages come from people rushing out patches. Either way, it's a really weird world right now, and it's time for us to get used to it.

I build AI for human intelligence. At Onix, we build small, private AI models from the knowledge of real health experts, so we protect their work and our users' health data every day. What scares me most is how unaware everybody is of this reality. These systems are alive and going at it all the time, everywhere.

Look at my own grandmother. Her email got hacked recently, and the whole family started getting messages from her address asking us to buy Amazon gift cards. A very standard hacking job. When we explained it to her, she didn't care. Not out of apathy. She didn't realize that her email is the central point for her family, her bank, her insurance and every other login. People don't see how tightly our accounts and identities are coupled. Getting into your email is like getting your fingerprint.

Nobody in my family got caught. We're all decently IT aware. But my brother did reply. He thought she wanted Amazon stuff and asked what she'd like for her birthday.

That scam was clumsy. AI changes that. The same technology that gives customer service fantastic digital twins, and lets movie studios generate whole scenes, is now in the hands of email scammers. And with automation and agents, one scammer can reach millions of people. A free, open model can [clone a voice from as little as 3 seconds of audio](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-TTS-12Hz-0.6B-Base). Look at all the videos people post of themselves and their kids on Instagram.

Scammers create panic on purpose. They flood your phone with password reset alerts, then call with a voice you know: "I'm in jail, wire me money." Over a grainy phone line, in a moment of dread, good enough is enough. Ask for the code word. An out-of-nowhere word like banana pancakes grounds you in the moment so you can ask: is this real?

We have to get back to analog ideas like this. Digital safety has to become what home ec or shop class used to be: basic, day-to-day practical skills that everyone learns.

Companies have a part too, starting at the root of our identities: our email and our phone providers. Piling on more PINs and passwords doesn't work, especially for older people. They end up reusing the same four-digit PIN for their bank, their card, their phone and their voicemail, and one breach opens everything. Companies need to be proactive. It costs money in the short term, but I can guarantee it pays back in happier customers, less fraud and less support overhead.

Governments should set a baseline. At Onix, we did HIPAA compliance and SOC 2. Why isn't there a straight-up mandate for baseline cybersecurity at every company? Encryption. Low data collection. If a company doesn't hold your data, it can't leak it. Why do they have all this information in the first place?

Stamos has also called for a "911 for internet crime" to fill the gap between local police and the FBI, staffed by people who are good online and would never pass a push-up test. This is the type of thinking we need, and more people should give it serious consideration. I'd extend it to Canada, between police and the RCMP, and down to the province and state level. A police department for the digital age. It's a bridge for young people looking for work who love computers, games and hacking. Digital safety is the new shop class, and protecting people online is a new trade job.

This Thanksgiving weekend, pick your code word over dessert. Don't play IT. Just talk about it. Go as deep as your family wants, and ask people how they feel.

Pick your banana pancakes. And bring the human back into the conversation.
